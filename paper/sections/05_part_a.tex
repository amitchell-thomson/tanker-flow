\section{Physical nowcasts}\label{sec:parta}

Before asking whether the signals predict a price, we ask whether they predict the
physical quantities they measure directly: weekly EU arrivals, weekly US loadings
and terminal outages, at one- and two-week horizons. These targets have high
signal-to-noise ratios and published ground truth, and they establish what the
pipeline can and cannot see before any price enters the analysis. Four models were
pre-registered and scored walk-forward against two naive nulls, persistence and a
four-week climatology. Each model was designed to address the diagnosed weakness of
the previous one. Table~\ref{tab:parta} gives the one-week results.

The sequence of results is informative. A1, a duration-climatology convolution of
the at-sea stock, is \aOneSkill{} worse than the climatology null; its estimator is
maturity-gated and therefore stale. A2, a negative-binomial count model on lagged
loadings and berth occupancy, is \aTwoSkill{} worse because it over-extrapolates
the trend. A2 also falsified one of its pre-registered signs: ballast arrivals
entered with coefficient \aTwoBallastCoef{} against a prior of positive. A4, a
Kalman local-level model, is \aFourSkill{} from the null, which we treat as a tie.
The tie carries information. A4 fitted its smoothing constant by maximum likelihood
and chose $\alpha=\aFourAlpha$, an effective window of \aFourWindow{} weeks. For a
series that is a local level plus noise, the exponentially weighted moving average
is the optimal linear predictor \citep{muth1960}. The fitted model landing on a
four-week simple average indicates that the optimum was found, and not that four
methods happened to tie. On this target and at this horizon, the naive mean is close
to optimal, and a fifth method would not have changed the conclusion.

\paragraph{Outage detection.} A5 tested Bayesian online change-point detection
\citep{adams2007} on the inter-departure process at each US terminal against
\aFiveOutages{} labelled outages over \aFiveTerminalYears{} terminal-years
(Table~\ref{tab:parta5}). It did not outperform a rate-relative silence rule, which
detects with a median delay of \aFiveNTwoDelay{} days at \aFiveNTwoFar{} false
alarms per terminal-year. The rule's recall is \aFiveNTwoRecall{}
(\aFiveNTwoHits). It is a usable but limited monitor, and it is the baseline rather
than the proposed model.

\paragraph{A withdrawn test.} A natural extension asks whether skill rises with
horizon, since cargo at sea today determines arrivals two to three weeks ahead. This
was pre-specified before any longer-horizon data was examined. Extending A1's window
to four weeks produced a \hOneSkillWFour{} improvement over the best null at week
four (Table~\ref{tab:h1}). The improvement is an artefact. A1's target is
conditional on legs already at sea, whereas both nulls are the last elapsed
unconditional weekly count. The two coincide at one and two weeks, because no US-to-EU
voyage completes in under seven days. Past the voyage tail the conditional truth
falls toward zero (mean \hOneTruthWOne{} arrivals in week one and
\hOneTruthWFour{} in week four) while the nulls continue to predict the full weekly
rate (\hOnePersistWFour{} MAE). A1 was forecasting a smaller quantity, not
forecasting better. The pre-specified premise, that the nulls degrade with horizon,
fails, and the protocol treats that outcome as grounds to abandon the test. In the
comparable region, skill moves from \hOneSkillWOne{} to \hOneSkillWTwo{}, which is
the predicted direction but remains below the null.
