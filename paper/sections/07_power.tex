\section{What the design could have found}\label{sec:power}

A null is only as informative as the smallest effect it could have detected. For a
partial effect estimated on $n$ observations, $t^2 \approx n\,R^2/(1-R^2)$, so at a
fixed bar the smallest detectable \pRsq{} falls as $1/n$. With \powerN{} discovery
weeks and the Bonferroni bar of $|t|>\bonferroniT$, the floor is \powerFloor{} of
residual variance (Figure~\ref{fig:power}). The largest \pRsq{} observed anywhere in
the paper --- across eight signals, two horizons, three mechanisms and two samples ---
is \bMaxRsqCov.

\label{fig:power}

The scope of the result follows. It rules out effects large enough to be worth
trading and is silent on smaller ones: a signal explaining a third of a percent of
residual variance would be real physics and a worthless strategy, and this design
could not distinguish it from zero. Two further scale facts make the null less
surprising than the intuition that motivated the study. The entire EU-bound stock of
gas at sea averages \stockMeanMm{} million\,m$^3$ of LNG, about \stockMeanBcm{}\,bcm
of gas or \stockDaysDemand{} days of EU demand at roughly \euDemandBcm{}\,bcm per
year; a one-standard-deviation swing in it is \stockSdDaysDemand{} days of demand.
The weekly spread, meanwhile, has a standard deviation of \$\spreadWeeklySd{} per
MMBtu. The question was whether a day and a half of marginal supply moves a price
that routinely swings on a revised weather forecast.
