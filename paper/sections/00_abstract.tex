\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Positions of liquefied natural gas carriers are widely believed to carry
information about the price gap between North American and European gas. A cargo
that leaves the US Gulf today lands in Europe in two to three weeks, and that
supply does not yet appear in any published statistic. We test this belief on the
transatlantic trade. From \nFixes{} AIS position fixes (\fixSpanLo{} to
\fixSpanHi{}) we reconstruct \nEvents{} port events at \nTerminals{} terminals and
a \nSignalKeys-signal daily panel, built once with hindsight for validation and
once strictly point-in-time for modelling. The US side of the reconstruction is
checked against EIA export totals. We then ask whether tanker-derived signals
predict the one- and four-week change in the \HH{}--\TTF{} spread after
controlling for its own persistence, weather, storage and oil. Each hypothesis,
sign and acceptance bar was recorded in a dated append-only log before the
corresponding fit. We find no predictive content. A controls-only model performs
worse than a no-change forecast (\bMTwoSkillHOneCov{} at one week,
\bMTwoSkillHFourCov{} at four). No signal reaches a Newey--West $|t|$ of 1.96
(maximum \bMaxTCov). Three pre-registered mechanisms that a linear scan could miss
(the Europe-bound share of cargo, an interaction with EU storage, and a
tightness-regime split) fail under a Bonferroni bar with an untouched holdout. The
design can detect effects of at least \powerFloor{} of residual variance; the
largest observed is \bMaxRsqCov. The result therefore excludes effects large enough
to trade and says nothing about smaller ones. Three things stand independently of
the null: a decade-scale event reconstruction with a measured capture rate, two
construction hazards specific to voyage-derived features (the unit of observation
is defined only in retrospect, and feature availability changes across data
vintages, so that a naive loader leaked \leakFutureShare{} of the sample), and a
physical-nowcast benchmark showing that a naive moving average is near-optimal for
weekly US loadings.
\end{abstract}
